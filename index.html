    About this page

    The network. The DWTClassifier of Prof. Chenhui Shao's group (University of Michigan): 528 wavelet features of an ultrasonic metal weld, hidden layers of 150, 100 and 50 ReLU units, and 9 output classes (3 tool states x 3 surface states). 99,959 parameters.

    Trained tab. One of the group's saved checkpoints (stratified split 0), trained on 162 labelled welds. The group's five saved networks score 88.9% ± 2.9% on their held-out test welds.

    Written by an LLM tab. The same architecture, with weights written directly as text by GPT-6 Astra from a description of the process, with no data and no training. This is the method of MIT CSAIL's "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming". Run on 2026-11-06: five replies, 2.2% to 14.4% test accuracy, 8.8% on average; guessing scores 11.1%.

    How it was written. For the LLM-written tab: the exact prompt, OpenAI's summaries of Astra's reasoning, Astra's plan, its edits group by group, the explanation it gave when asked two days later, and the rule decoded from the finished weights. The reasoning itself was not returned by the API: the summaries are a summarizer's paraphrase of it, and the later explanation is after the fact, not a trace.

    Written from 18 welds tab. Dennis's experiment (branch dennis/usmw-few-welds): not the MLP, but a centroid per condition and a spread per feature on 8 features, written by Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 from 18 labelled welds. About 70% on the 252 held-out welds, against 69% for a formula with no AI.

    MIT's own networks tab. The networks of MIT CSAIL's "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming" itself, exactly as MIT logged them (RikiyaT/lm_understand-logs, CC BY 4.0). Experiment 5: the MinAtar Breakout, Seaquest and Asterix policies (600 → 32 → 32 → 6) that GPT-6 Astra wrote from each game's rules and source code. Experiment 4: a Kinetix car policy trained by PPO that never reaches its goal, and the same policy after Astra repaired it in one reply. Each reply's statements, replayed with the research repository's reader, rebuild MIT's network exactly. The game screens come from episodes played here with MIT's networks and MinAtar's own code as MIT's prompts quote it; the car's from the replication's Kinetix environment.

    Which inputs matter tab. Not a network: charts of an exploratory analysis in the research repository (experiments/usmw_feature_usefulness, 2026-11-16). Logistic regression and the lasso on Prof. Shao's 528 inputs, one question at a time (three pairs of tools, three pairs of surfaces, all 9 conditions), on the group's 5 splits; what the trained networks lose when groups of inputs are shuffled; and time-window inputs for comparison. Every chart shows held-out test accuracy or how often an input was picked; the window statistics were chosen after looking at the labels, so their accuracy is optimistic.

    Course methods tab. Not a network: what the ME 499/599 lectures on feature selection and classification (Topics 9 to 12, Lab 4) show on the 270 welds (experiments/usmw_course_methods, 2026-11-17). When each weld was made; Fisher's ratio for each feature, pooled and per tool; how much Prof. Shao's 12 statistics repeat each other; Lab 4's screen-then-LDA/QDA procedure; Prof. Shao's residuals inside each tool session; and eight classifiers on the same splits. Exploratory: every model uses all 162 training welds, and no LLM is involved.

    World model tab. The world-model pilot of the research repository (experiments/usmw_world_model_pilot, 2026-11-18, exploratory), run live in the page. A transformer encoder (4 layers, 172,480 weights) learned from 216 unlabeled welds to predict hidden 25 ms patches of a weld from the rest (a JEPA); then a logistic-regression head was trained on its frozen 128-number summary of each weld, with k labeled welds per condition. The page holds the encoder, its predictor and its slowly updated copy at 9 points of training on split 0 (re-run bit for bit from the pilot's seeds), 18 test welds, and the heads; every heatmap is computed in the browser and checked against PyTorch's outputs. Over 5 splits, the trained encoder scored no better than the same encoder with random weights.

    Inputs. The real welds are held-out test welds of split 0, standardized with the checkpoint's own scaler. Nine were picked among the welds the trained network reads correctly and one among those it misreads, so they are not a fair sample of its accuracy. The synthetic inputs are not welds: each is the smallest pattern the trained network reads as one class with 99% confidence.

    Private. The weights, the weld features and the prompts (which hold weld values) come from a course dataset that is not public. Please do not pass this page, its link or its data files on outside the team. The published copy is locked: it opens only from the full link.
